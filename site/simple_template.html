The Traffic Guessing Game

Our project teaches computers to guess how many cars will pass a spot on the road 15 minutes from now.

A simple explanation of "An Enhanced Machine Learning Framework for Short-Term Traffic Flow Forecasting" by Ankit Bharatula and Ansh Singh, SRM Institute of Science and Technology.

1Why guess traffic?

If we know a road will be busy soon, traffic lights can change earlier and maps can send cars another way. That means less waiting in traffic jams.

2Where do the numbers come from?

California (USA) has little car-counting machines built into its big roads. Every 5 minutes, each machine writes down how many cars went past it. We used a famous public dataset called PeMSD4.

4Practice, mock test, final exam

Like school, we cut the 59 days into three parts, in time order:

Practice 70%
Mock 15%
Exam 15%

Practice (first 41 days): the computer learns. Mock test (next 9 days): we pick each player's best settings. Final exam (last 9 days): answers stay hidden and each player takes the exam only once.

The no-cheating rule

Nobody can peek at the future. When guessing, a player may only use counts it could really know by that time. We even wrote a checking program (an "audit") that confirms nobody cheated.

5Meet the 7 players

Seven different "guessers" played the same game on the same exam.

Say it in one minute

"Our project guesses how many cars will pass a road 15 minutes from now.

We used real counts from 307 car-counting machines in California, taken every 5 minutes for 59 days.

The computer looks at the last one hour and guesses 15 minutes ahead. We trained on the early days, chose settings on the middle days, and tested once on the last 9 days, without peeking at the future.

We compared 7 guessers. Our idea joins an LSTM, which remembers patterns, with XGBoost, which fixes mistakes. It was a little better than XGBoost but much slower. A graph model called STGCN, which also watches neighbouring roads, did best.

So: looking at the road network helps the most, and our hybrid gives only a small gain."

Easy answers to hard questions

What does MAE mean?

"Mean Absolute Error": on average, how many cars our guess was away from the real count. If we guess 100 and the real count is 110, we are 10 off.

Why 15 minutes?

It is short-term forecasting: close enough to be accurate, and early enough to change traffic lights or routes. 15 minutes = 3 counts of 5 minutes.

How do you know you didn't cheat?

Data is split by time, the final exam was used only once, settings were chosen on the mock test, and no guess uses a count from the future. An audit program checks all of this and says PASS.

Your own model isn't the best. Is that bad?

No. Research must be honest. We report what really happened: our hybrid gave a small gain, and STGCN did better. That is a useful finding.

What are RMSE and MAPE?

RMSE is like MAE, but punishes big mistakes more. MAPE is the mistake as a percentage of the real count. For all three, smaller is better.

Can I see it working?

Yes. The results website shows real guesses from every player, and the Streamlit app makes a new 15-minute guess when you press a button.
